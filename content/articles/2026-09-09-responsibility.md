  How can we act rightly when we no longer truly believe in Good and Evil?

  After searching for answers in morality, in relations of power, and then in an almost unconditional form of kindness, one idea gradually imposed itself on me: responsibility.

  Not being responsible for everything that happens, but for what we choose to understand, for our intentions, for the consequences we can anticipate, and for the unnecessary suffering we can prevent.

  An attempt to build a morality without dogma, somewhere between our powerlessness and our capacity to act.

For several years now, I have stopped believing in morality. Or, more precisely, I have stopped believing in a certain idea of morality.

It has always seemed deceptive to me. It changes shape, stretches, and adapts itself to contexts, cultures, eras, and each person’s own sense of truth. What seems right here becomes reprehensible elsewhere; what seemed good yesterday can sometimes appear monstrous to us today. More troubling still, morality always carries within it the possibility of evil convinced of its own goodness—of someone who acts with the certainty of being right and who, precisely for that reason, no longer doubts himself.

For a long time, Charles Péguy’s famous phrase made me laugh: “Kantian morality has clean hands, but it has no hands.”

I liked that sentence because it gave morality all its humanity. It perfectly describes that aspect of human nature which strives toward perfection while keeping its feet in the mud, condemned to wrestle with its own contradictions.

At the time, I believed that morality could be arranged into a hierarchy.

An action was good first and foremost because of its consequences, even when it was driven by dogma, expectations, or imperfect motives. Then came a higher form: an action pure in its intention, even when external factors or an incomplete understanding of the situation led to a mediocre outcome.

Finally, the “beautiful” action was the one that managed to unite the two: pure in intention and good in its consequences.

But this entire construction was constantly threatened by one very simple question: what is good?

And what is evil?

If these two notions change across cultures, individuals, and eras, how can we build upon them something solid enough to guide a life?

I therefore had to abandon this idea and change paradigms.

I then drank deeply from Nietzsche’s words. Morality came to seem to me like an impoverished vision of the world, or at least one insufficient to understand it. Above all, it caused me suffering, particularly when the movement was from the world toward me.

I found it very difficult to move beyond the suffering caused by someone whose behaviour I considered immoral. I struggled with injustice, incivility, rudeness, or a lack of consideration. Each time, my moral judgment added something to the wound: not only did what had happened hurt me, but it should never have happened.

It seemed more peaceful, then, to accept the world as it was and to view it through relations of power.

It was no longer a matter of morality, but of beings seeking to exist, to assert themselves, to extend their influence, consciously or unconsciously, whether or not they considered the good they might bring to those around them.

This way of thinking helped me accept the world more fully.

But it raised a new problem.

If it could help me understand the movement from the world toward me, what about the movement from me toward the world?

If I accepted that the world was shaped by relations of power, and that the notions of good and evil were profoundly dependent on our perspectives, I no longer knew how I ought to act toward it.

How can one live with integrity when one no longer knows exactly what it means to “be good”?

So I made myself a promise:

Each day, I would try to choose the intention and the action that would avoid, as far as possible, causing unnecessary suffering around me. In return, I would accept betrayal, injustice, and hurt as consequences of my inability to control the world.

Put simply: be good to the world, but expect only the worst from it.

In theory, this seemed wise to me. I already imagined myself as one of those black holes for hatred, capable of turning fear into love, violence into gentleness, and stupidity into acts of grace.

In practice, the path was far more difficult.

My human flaws tolerated this theory less and less. At best, I ended up wishing indifference upon those who had hurt me; at worst, I wished them misfortune and suffering.

Conversely, in my deepest moments of kindness, this philosophy sometimes led me to forgive everything, accept everything, and let everything pass.

And I was deeply disappointed in myself when I realized that this kindness was sometimes merely a more flattering portrait of my lack of courage.

I did not always forgive because I was good.

Sometimes I forgave because I lacked the courage to stand up for myself.

Kindness without courage could become cowardice.

Once again, I had to change paradigms.

There were certain things I wanted to keep.

I still liked the idea of acting with the right intention while trying to anticipate the consequences of my actions. I also liked distinguishing between how I behaved toward the world and what I expected from it.

But two problems remained.

First, the notions of good and evil were still unclear.

Second, accepting other people’s behaviour without ever responding to it seemed just as wrong. It almost implied that some external form of justice would eventually restore the balance: life, fate, God, karma, or some other mysterious force charged with giving everyone what they deserve.

I could not believe in that kind of justice.

Sometimes, one has to resist. To say no. To set a boundary. To accept conflict. And perhaps even to accept causing pain when no possible solution can avoid suffering altogether.

To this difficulty was added another, more ordinary one.

We are constantly affiliated with schools of thought and with ideological, political, philosophical, or social groups. As soon as we agree with one idea defended by a group, we are easily judged as though we subscribed to every idea held by that same group. And when we try to introduce nuance or criticism into our own positions, that nuance is sometimes interpreted as a lack of integrity, courage, or conviction.

As though we had to choose a side before choosing our ideas.

But I no longer wanted to delegate my judgment to a morality, an ideology, a group, or a philosophy.

That was when individual responsibility appeared to me as a possible answer.

Its principle is simple: society is built through the accumulation of our individual choices. To live together as a society therefore requires us to become aware—or at least to strive toward awareness—of the responsibility we bear for our own lives and for the small portion of the world that our actions can reach.

We are responsible for the attention we give to the world and to those around us.

We are responsible, insofar as we can be, for the intentions we put into our actions.

We are responsible for the effort we make to anticipate their consequences.

And we are responsible for our ability to question our own certainties.

This obviously does not mean that we control everything.

We never hold all the cards. We never possess a perfect understanding of a situation. Some external factors are unpredictable, and our conditioning can sometimes be profoundly difficult to identify, confront, and transform.

We are therefore not responsible for everything that happens.

But between absolute control and total powerlessness lies a margin in which we can act.

And it is precisely within that margin that responsibility begins, for me.

To pretend that it does not exist—that everything is predetermined by our conditioning, our impulses, or our environment—is to surrender an essential part of our capacity to act.

Many of our actions arise from our desires. But it is up to us, as far as possible, to pass those desires through the filter of awareness and reason.

“Taking responsibility” then becomes something accessible to everyone.

We can first take responsibility for listening to ourselves and trying to understand ourselves: our history, our emotions, our feelings, our moods, our thoughts, our reactions, and our desires.

Then we can take responsibility for examining the consequences of our everyday actions: their impact on others, on our environment, on society, and on nature.

We can then take responsibility for our intentions by honestly asking ourselves what motivates our actions.

Finally, we can sharpen the way we look at and listen to the world, so that we learn to anticipate the existence of others before we even act.

For me, this is the definition of consideration: making room for another person in our decision before that decision reaches them.

There remains, then, the question that had caused everything else to falter: the question of good and evil.

I still do not know how to define them.

But suffering, at least, possesses a reality that evil does not: it can be experienced.

This does not mean that all suffering is bad.

Some forms of suffering are inevitable: illness, ageing, separation, death.

There are forms of suffering that we may choose or accept because they accompany something we consider worth pursuing: effort, failure, learning, grief, a difficult truth, the courage to leave or to stand against something.

These forms of suffering are not “made” to help us grow. The world may have no intention toward us at all. But sometimes, we can grow through them.

And then there are forms of suffering that nothing seems sufficient to justify: gratuitous humiliation, cruelty, mockery, unnecessary violence, imposed loneliness, contempt, deliberate indifference to pain that we could easily have prevented.

I therefore choose, as far as possible, to replace the notion of evil with that of unnecessary suffering.

But the word “unnecessary” is essential.

Because being responsible does not mean never causing suffering.

Telling the truth can cause suffering. Setting a boundary can cause suffering. Refusing something can cause suffering. Standing against someone can cause suffering. Sometimes, every option available to us contains some degree of suffering.

Responsibility, therefore, does not mean remaining innocent.

It means accepting the burden of judgment.

It means looking at a situation as lucidly as possible, questioning our motivations, anticipating the consequences we are capable of foreseeing, listening to those whom our choices affect, and then acting—and accepting responsibility for our decision.

And so I no longer seek a morality that would allow me to distinguish definitively between Good and Evil.

Instead, I seek to become responsible.

Responsible for my own development, so that I may better understand what drives me.

Responsible for my intentions, so that I do not disguise my desires behind noble justifications.

Responsible for my actions and for the consequences I could reasonably have anticipated.

Responsible for my environment, by remaining attentive and open to what exists beyond myself.

And responsible, finally, for preserving enough courage to resist when acceptance becomes cowardice, and enough doubt to question myself when certainty becomes blindness.

I am not responsible for everything that happens.

I am responsible for what I seek to understand, for what motivates my actions, for what I could reasonably have foreseen, for the suffering I could have prevented, and for the way I respond to the suffering I could not prevent.

It is within this narrow space, between our powerlessness and our power, that I now place my morality.
