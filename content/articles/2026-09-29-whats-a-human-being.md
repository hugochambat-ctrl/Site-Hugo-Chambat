# What Is a Human Being?

Defining the human being is probably an undertaking doomed to remain incomplete. We could define ourselves through biology, behaviour, societies, or history. But if I had to search for what characterises our particular way of existing in the world, I would begin with three principles: reflective consciousness, pathos, and logos.

## Reflective Consciousness

Human beings are not merely conscious: they are conscious of being conscious. They can take themselves as an object of thought, observe their own thoughts, question their actions, and even question their own existence.

And with this consciousness comes a singular knowledge: the knowledge of our finitude.

I am.
I know that I am.
I know that I will cease to be.

And so a question arises: what should I do with the time in between?

Reflective consciousness thus makes the metaphysical question possible. If my existence is limited, what does it mean? Am I free to shape it, or am I subject to a destiny beyond my control? And if I desperately search for a meaning that the world refuses to give me, how am I to live with the absurd?

Perhaps it is from this strange condition of being alive while knowing that we are going to die; that some of our deepest questions about meaning, freedom, destiny, and spirituality arise.

## Pathos

But human beings are not merely beings who watch themselves live. They want.

They are driven by desires, passions, impulses, fears, and ambitions that set them in motion. Here, I will use *pathos* to describe all of these forces that compel us to act.

I see three main directions within them.

The first is the desire to expand: to conquer, dominate, possess, create, be recognised, leave a mark.

The second is the desire to know: that Prometheus flame that drives us to explore what lies beyond the mountain, to understand the movement of the stars, to open up a body, cross an ocean, or search for the particles that make up matter.

The third is the desire to enjoy and endure: to seek pleasure, flee pain, reproduce, survive, and conserve our energy. There is even an astonishing creative power in our laziness: human beings display extraordinary ingenuity in order to achieve as much (or even more) while making less effort.

Perhaps our machines tell us as much about our intelligence as they do about our laziness.

## Logos

Finally, human beings possess *logos*.

The Greek word is difficult to confine to a single translation. It refers at once to speech, discourse, reason, and reasoning. I use it here to describe our particular capacity to transform the world into thought and signs.

Human beings do not merely experience reality: they can represent it symbolically.

They can name what is absent, tell the story of what has disappeared, imagine what has never existed. They can express their sensations, communicate their desires, construct concepts, transmit visions, and even attempt to look at the world while abstracting themselves from it.

Stranger still, they can create worlds that do not exist.

Perhaps this is where language, philosophy, science, myth, and art are born together: different ways of taking reality, representing it, and sometimes transcending it.

## I Think, I Want, I Know

These three principles, however, do not exist separately.

When pathos says, “I want,” logos replies, “I think about what I want,” and reflective consciousness adds:

“I know that it is I who wants and thinks; and I know that all of this will end.”

We can desire something that our reason condemns. We can understand perfectly well what would be good for us and do precisely the opposite. We can seek pleasure while knowing that it will destroy us. We can want to be free while simultaneously longing for security. We can even watch ourselves enact our own contradictions, perfectly aware of their absurdity.

Here, then, is my conclusion: perhaps the human being is not found solely in pathos, logos, or reflective consciousness, but in the tragic and permanent tension between the three.

We desire.
We think about our desires.
We watch ourselves think and desire.

And somewhere within this strange movement, knowing that our time is limited, we try to live.
