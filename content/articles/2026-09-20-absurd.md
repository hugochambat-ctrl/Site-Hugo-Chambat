  What do we do when the world suddenly ceases to make sense?

  Through Camus and the experience of existential crisis, this article explores the absurd: the vertigo born from the confrontation between our need for meaning and the silence of the world.

Like many of us, I feel that I experienced the absurd long before I ever read anything about it.

It often emerges during periods of existential crisis.

It appears when the meaning of our lives begins to slip away beneath our feet. When the values we believed in collapse, when our dreams become unreachable, when the people we love leave, when the places we inhabit disappear, when a part of ourselves dies.

Little by little, something begins to detach itself.

The events that once seemed so important lose some of their weight. Our hopes, our ambitions, even our suffering are viewed from a greater distance. As though stepping back were a survival mechanism. A way of suffering less by ceasing to believe that all of this matters quite so much.

And behind this detachment, a question eventually emerges:

**What’s the point?**

I discovered Camus after I had already felt the weight of that question.

He defines the absurd as the confrontation between the human need for meaning and the silence of the world.

We need to give meaning to our actions, our lives and, perhaps more than anything else, our suffering. We build values, we tell stories, we search for causes and consequences. We want things to mean something.

But the world guarantees us none of this.

Unlike the stories we love to tell, it promises neither justice, nor morality, nor resolution. It does not guarantee that good people will be rewarded, that our suffering will serve some purpose, or that what we love will ever be returned to us.

And when we ask, “Why?”

The world does not answer.

It is within this silence that the absurd is born.

Faced with it, Camus explores several possibilities. First, suicide, which he rejects because it eliminates one side of the problem (the human being) rather than confronting the contradiction itself. Then comes the appeal to transcendence, when it becomes a way of artificially resolving what existence itself leaves unanswered.

Ultimately, he proposes another path: not to resolve the absurd, but to live with it.

Like Sisyphus eternally pushing his rock, the absurd man finds his freedom, his greatness, and even his joy in the lucid awareness of his condition.

Existential crisis is the precise moment when the absurd ceases to be an idea and becomes an experience.

It can arise from a deep sense of weariness. It can also strike suddenly: a separation, a failure, a death, the disappearance of a dream, or sometimes simply the vague feeling that something no longer holds together.

Then the questions return:

**Why? What’s the point?**

We all play the game of life knowing, somewhere within ourselves, that we are moving inexorably toward death. And when the structures that allowed us to forget this begin to collapse, a strange feeling can emerge: the feeling of becoming a stranger to our own reality. Sometimes, a stranger to our own life.

Camus was one of the first philosophers who truly helped me. He gave words to feelings I had struggled to name.

And he helped me understand that the absence of an answer might not be the end of the problem. It might be its beginning.

Existential crises will probably come several times throughout a lifetime. They will return for as long as we continue to believe, to love, and to build.

Because we are builders in a world where everything we build will eventually disappear.

The people we love. The places we inhabit. Our certainties. Our dreams. Ourselves.

And sometimes, when these things cease to exist, the meaning we had given them disappears with them.

Existential crisis therefore seems to me inseparable from the human condition. It is one of its necessary forms of suffering.

Necessary, first, in an almost philosophical sense: it cannot *not* exist, because as long as we need meaning in a world that guarantees us none, the confrontation seems inevitable.

But necessary also in the ordinary sense of the word: necessary because it destroys.

It destroys certain certainties, certain values, certain stories we used to tell ourselves about who we are and about the world.

And when something has been destroyed, a new question appears:

**What do I want to rebuild?**

This is where a form of freedom begins.

Not the absolute freedom of an individual who could somehow escape their history, their body, their environment, or their conditioning. But the freedom to look at what we have been given, at what we have believed until now, and consciously decide what we want to keep, what we want to abandon, and what we want to build.

It is probably in this sense that I feel close to existentialism.

Experiencing the absurd is no longer merely a loss of meaning. It becomes the starting point from which we can choose our own path.

The world may never tell me what is worth living for.

It will not tell me what is worth loving.

It will not tell me what is worth building.

So I am left to choose.

And if no meaning is given to me, then I am left to decide what I want to give meaning to.

And so, I choose beauty to give meaning to my life.
